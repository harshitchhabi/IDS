\subsection{The policy-consistency blind spot}
% DECISIONS.md s21.1, s23: loss-based filtering (out-of-fold XGBoost, 3-fold, honeypot rows
% with p(stamped label) < 0.1 get weight 0) fails wherever there is damage -- its zero-weight
% fraction falls as poison density rises (60% dropped at 5% poison, 33% at 10%, 1.5% at 20%,
% 0% at 50%), because a dense block of identically-stamped rows teaches the auxiliary model
% its own label, so out-of-fold loss stops flagging it. This is NOT because the poison is
% "correctly labelled": it is wrong against ground truth (benign rows stamped malicious) but
% consistent with the defender's own labelling policy, and dense enough to be self-consistent.
A generic defense that checks a honeypot label's consistency against the loop's own data fails
here, and not because the label is correct. A1's poison is wrong against ground truth — benign
rows stamped malicious — but it is \emph{consistent with the defender's own labelling policy},
and once there is enough of it, that consistency is self-reinforcing: a model fit on the loop's
data learns the stamped label as its own. Loss-based filtering (an auxiliary model's out-of-fold
probability of the stamped label, dropping rows it scores low) demonstrates exactly this
failure mode: it drops 60\% of poison at 5\% ratio, 33\% at 10\%, 1.5\% at 20\%, and 0\% at 50\%
— it weakens precisely as the poison becomes dense enough to matter, and recovers essentially
none of the damage anywhere damage exists (Table~\ref{tab:loss-filter}). It is not that the
poison is correctly labelled; it is that a filter trained on the loop's own labels cannot see
past a label the loop itself supplied consistently.
% DECISIONS.md s21.1: kNN sanitization (k=10 against the TRUSTED seed set, drop if fewer than
% half neighbours agree with the stamped label) does NOT fail, because it anchors outside the
% loop -- to seed_train's ground-truth labels, not the loop's own poisoned data. It drops 98.5%
% of A1 poison. This falsifies the blanket claim "generic defenses fail because poison is
% policy-consistent" -- the failure is specific to defenses that learn from the poisoned data.
kNN sanitization, anchored instead to the trusted \texttt{seed\_train} labels rather than to
the loop's own data, does not fail this way: it drops 98.5\% of A1's poison, because it can see
that the poison sits among \emph{benign} seed neighbours despite carrying a malicious stamp.
The blanket claim "generic defenses fail because the poison is policy-consistent" is therefore
false — the failure is specific to defenses that learn from the data the loop itself produced,
not to label-consistency checking in general.

\subsection{D1: cost-of-influence weighting}
% DECISIONS.md s20: D1 weights a honeypot row's sample_weight by attacker effort,
% w = min(1, (e/E*)^gamma), e = geometric-mean of (1 + cost_k / median_benign_cost_k) - 1
% over (duration, packets, bytes, protocol-state depth). Pre-registered default E*=8, gamma=2,
% fixed before any D1 result or implementation.
D1 prices a honeypot row's influence on the retrained model by what it cost the attacker to
produce. Each row's \emph{effort} is a geometric mean, over duration, packet count, byte count
and protocol-state depth, of that row's cost relative to a median trusted benign flow's cost in
the same component; its \texttt{sample\_weight} saturates to 1 once effort clears a threshold
$E^*$ and is 0 for a free interaction. $E^*=8,\gamma=2$ — full influence costs roughly an order
of magnitude more than a typical legitimate flow — was fixed, in DECISIONS.md, before D1 was
implemented or any defended run existed.
% DECISIONS.md s20 "Sensitivity protocol (pre-registered)": "not sensitive" iff A1 FPR damage
% stays within 2 sigma_control over the region E*>=4, gamma>=1. s21.5: met in 2 of 12
% configurations (E*=32, g in {2,3}); damage is monotone in both parameters; every
% configuration in the region still recovers >=86% of undefended damage, but the residual at
% 50% poison scales with E* and gamma and is not negligible. Criterion recorded as failed, not
% revised after the fact (DECISIONS.md s25.1(3)).
D1 was pre-registered to be judged against its own stated criterion: "not sensitive" if A1's
FPR damage stays within $2\sigma$ of the control's noise floor across $E^*\ge4,\gamma\ge1$.
\textbf{That criterion was not met.} Only 2 of the 12 configurations in that region satisfy it
(both at $E^*=32$); damage is monotone in both parameters, and while every configuration still
removes at least 86\% of the undefended damage, the residual at 50\% poison scales with $E^*$
and $\gamma$ rather than vanishing. This is reported as a failed pre-registered test, and the
criterion is not revised after seeing the result.

\subsection{The frontier, and the right no-skill baseline}
% DECISIONS.md s24.1: XGBoost, 5 seeds, tau=0.1. Recovery = A1 raw fidelity, ratio 0.5 (0.9
% where run), fixed threshold. Retention = share of undefended S0 honeypot_only gain kept,
% synthetic (0.05/0.2) and CICIDS (0.2). Finding 1: no D1 setting dominates kNN. Finding 3:
% fixed-weight uniform down-weighting matches kNN at ratio 0.5 (recovery 98.9-99.8%, better
% retention) but collapses at ratio 0.9 (43-52% vs kNN/D1q's 99%) -- it caps effective share
% below the damage onset only while the nominal ratio is itself below that cap's regime.
Recovery ($R$) and retention ($U$, Section~\ref{sec:methodology}) place every defense on one
frontier (Figure~\ref{fig:f6-frontier}). No setting of pre-registered D1 dominates kNN
sanitization on this grid; the pre-registered point itself is the worst tested on synthetic
retention. A defense using no cost information at all — a fixed weight on every honeypot row —
is a real comparator here, not a strawman: at poison ratio 0.5 it matches kNN's recovery
(98.9--99.8\%) while keeping more of the honest loop's gain. It stops working once the poison
ratio itself rises: at ratio 0.9 fixed uniform weighting recovers only 43--52\%, where kNN and
the best D1q setting both recover \textasciitilde99\%. A defense that must work at a poison
ratio the defender does not control cannot be a single fixed weight; it has to respond to how
much of the training set the honeypot actually is.
% DECISIONS.md s25.2-25.3, s25.5: ShareCap caps the honeypot's EFFECTIVE share of the training
% set at c (recomputed every retrain), rather than every row's weight. It is undominated by
% every D1 variant, kNN and fixed uniform weight in all 87 (setting, retention-axis)
% comparisons, and protects at both ratios where fixed uniform weighting does not -- because it
% responds to the actual poison share rather than assuming one. The full cap curve (Figure 6,
% bottom row / s25.5) shows this protection holds through c<=0.10 and degrades between 0.10 and
% 0.20 for this model and dataset; there is no cap that is simultaneously maximally protective
% and maximally retentive.
\textbf{ShareCap} — cap the honeypot's \emph{effective} share of the training set at $c$,
recomputed every retrain, rather than fix every row's weight — responds to exactly the quantity
fixed uniform weighting cannot see. It is \textbf{undominated} by every D1 variant, kNN and
fixed uniform weighting across all 87 (setting, retention-axis) comparisons tested, and it
protects at both poison ratios tested, where fixed uniform weighting does not
(Figure~\ref{fig:f6-frontier}, bottom row). This is not a claim that ShareCap beats every other
defense in every comparison — undominated means no alternative is at least as good on both
recovery and retention, not that ShareCap is strictly better on each axis individually. The cap
itself is a tuning knob: the full cap-sensitivity curve holds through roughly $c\le0.10$ for
this model and dataset and degrades between $c=0.10$ and $c=0.20$; there is no cap that is
simultaneously maximally protective and maximally retentive, and the safe range is
model-and-dataset-dependent, not a universal constant.
% DECISIONS.md s21.3, s25.1: D1 overhead 0.000216 s/round vs kNN 0.168 s/round at ratio 0.5,
% 781x (95% bootstrap 735-837x); ShareCap has no cost model and no auxiliary computation, so
% its per-round cost is dominated by the same weight recomputation D1 already does.
D1's measured overhead advantage stands regardless of the dominance result: 0.000216 s per
round against kNN's 0.168 s at ratio 0.5, a 781$\times$ ratio (95\% bootstrap 735--837$\times$).
ShareCap's overhead is of the same order as D1's — a weight recomputation, no auxiliary model,
no per-row distance query — and needs no cost model, no trusted reference beyond the cap value
itself, and no knowledge of the poison ratio.
% DECISIONS.md s21.4, s21.6: attacker cost to reach the same damage target under D1 rises
% 4x-36x in flows (bracketed by the ratio grid). Padding (scaling duration/packets/bytes) raises
% D1's weight on poison but moves it off copy fidelity, where A1's damage already lives -- so
% padding fails against the UNDEFENDED loop too, and is not itself evidence for D1: the
% fidelity break does that on its own. The attacker's real counter-move is volume, not padding.
Against D1 specifically, an attacker forced to keep copy-level fidelity (where A1's damage
lives, Section~\ref{sec:attack}) cannot pad individual flows to buy weight, because padding
moves a flow away from the copy-level fidelity the damage depends on — a padded attack fails
against the \emph{undefended} loop too, so padding is not itself evidence for D1's protection.
The available counter-move is volume: reaching the same damage under D1 costs 4--36$\times$
more flows, bracketed by the ratio grid tested (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}).
